The Challenger 650 is Bombardier's flagship mid-size business jet — the most evolved model in a lineage stretching back to the Challenger 600 of 1978. The 650 variant, introduced in 2015, features a redesigned interior with one of the widest cabins in its class, a Rockwell Collins ProLine 21 Advanced Flight Deck, and more powerful engines than its predecessor the Challenger 605.

With a range of approximately 7,400 kilometres, the aircraft can fly non-stop from Toronto to London Heathrow, or reach most destinations in the continental United States and Canada without refuelling.

The Ontario government purchased this specific aircraft directly from Bombardier. It was formerly owned by an entity in South America. Both the Ontario Provincial Police and Bombardier performed security and mechanical inspections prior to purchase. The province says no upgrades were required.

Context & Background

The Gravy Train promise

The irony driving opposition outrage is impossible to miss. Doug Ford built much of his political brand — inherited from his brother, the late Toronto mayor Rob Ford — on attacking government waste, or as the Fords put it, the "gravy train." When Ontario's NDP leader Marit Stiles labelled the jet the "Gravy Plane," she was twisting Ford's own language back at him.

As recently as 2019, Ford publicly pushed back on using a government aircraft at all, saying he would rather drive across the province. His office instead kitted out a custom Ontario Provincial Police van — complete with a 32-inch Blu-ray television, mini fridge, and leather power-reclining sofa — which drew its own wave of ridicule. The NDP dubbed it the "pleasure wagon."

The government's defence is that a premier's time has value, Ontario is geographically massive, and the per-jet cost compares favourably with Quebec's $107M fleet and the federal government's $753M fleet of six Global 6500 jets. Ford's office also insists the plane will only be used for government business, not personal travel.

Critics, however, point to the timing: the purchase was confirmed the same week Ford's government is pursuing plans to take over Billy Bishop Toronto City Airport and allow private jets to land there — a facility currently restricted to propeller aircraft. NoJetsTO, a citizen group opposing the takeover, alleged the two moves are "not a coincidence."

Perhaps the most pointed irony was the original entry-into-service date: the plane was expected to be operational by the end of July — the height of cottage season. Ford is well known to spend significant portions of the summer at his cottage in the Muskokas. Muskoka Airport (YQA), a jet-capable airport near Bracebridge roughly 15 minutes from cottage country, is equipped to receive the Challenger 650. That plan has now been abandoned following the reversal — but the plane remains in Canada, and a charter arrangement could still achieve the same result. Whether C-FBBW, or any aircraft chartered by the Ontario government, appears at YQA on a summer weekend remains a question worth asking.

There is one more layer of irony worth noting. Ford has publicly acknowledged a fear of flying — specifically of small propeller aircraft. During press conferences with Northern Ontario First Nations communities, he explained that turbulence on single-engine prop planes left him gripping his seat in terror. The Challenger 650, with its large cabin, twin jet engines, and smooth high-altitude cruise, is as far from a single-prop bush plane as aviation gets. The $28.9M purchase may be, at least in part, a very expensive solution to a personal anxiety.

"I'm terrified when I go on those things."

— Premier Doug Ford, on single-propeller aircraft, November 2025

"You hit wind turbulence, and you're — at least my heart — it just drops, and you drop 100 feet, and you're holding on for dear life."

— Premier Doug Ford, October 2025

The U-turn — April 19, 2026

Ford backs down — selling "as quickly as possible"

Just 48 hours after the Ontario government confirmed the $28.9 million jet purchase, Premier Doug Ford announced a full reversal. In a statement released Sunday morning, Ford said the province would move to sell the aircraft immediately, working with Bombardier and "other partners" to find a buyer.

Ford's statement acknowledged the public backlash directly: "Despite the best of intentions, I have heard and agree that now is not the right time for the expense of a government plane." He did not address the costs already incurred, or what price the province expects to achieve on the resale.

The reversal raises serious questions about the true cost of the fiasco to Ontario taxpayers. The purchase price was $28.9 million, but that is almost certainly not the full story. The plane has already undergone OPP security and mechanical inspections, been flown from the United States to Toronto, and is presumably sitting in a hangar at or near Pearson. Selling a used business jet is not instantaneous — broker fees, carrying costs, and any difference between the purchase price and resale price will all land on the public tab.

The broader political damage is harder to quantify. Ford built his brand on fiscal restraint and fighting government waste — the "gravy train" was his signature attack line. Spending $28.9M on a private jet, then reversing course two days later under pressure, raises questions about the judgement of both the original decision and the process that led to it. How did a $28.9M purchase go from "months in the making" to "selling as quickly as possible" in 48 hours?

"Despite the best of intentions, I have heard and agree that now is not the right time for the expense of a government plane. The province is working with Bombardier and other partners to sell the plane as quickly as possible."

— Premier Doug Ford, statement, April 19, 2026
